correlated, and a tight bound on their total overflow remains
an open problem. NanoGRAM, whose pile collects overflow
from all levels, sizes the pile by empirical simulation. We
instead give each stage its own stash and combine the stages
with a union bound (Appendix A).
The union bound is loose, but lookups never scan the stage
stashes, which would add 20 stashes to every lookup in a table
of 220 buckets. Most residuals overflowed at an intermediate
stage, although their destination bucket has room, so a final
sweep over the output table gives them a second chance (Fig-
ure 4). Writing a residual directly into its destination would
reveal that bucket. The merge instead obliviously partitions
the residuals by destination into one range bucket for each
range of consecutive output buckets, and the sweep reads the
range bucket at every output bucket in its range, whether or
not any residual belongs there. The few records that still do
not fit form the new table’s residual tier, which a lookup
scans in full whenever it probes the table. As a result, the tier
is as small as any placement of the table’s keys into 64-slot
buckets can make it, and we show one or two buckets suffice
for each tier in our default configuration (Appendix A). The
tier is offered to the sweep again at the table’s next merge,
where a different hash is used.
Duplicate keys and the stash bound. The stash bound of
each stage requires the routed records to have distinct keys.
Because our hash tables are read-only, however, an access
leaves the older copy of its key in place, so the two tables of
a merge can hold copies of the same key. At first glance, this
seems harmless: both copies carry the same routing tag, so
they meet in the merge’s last stage, whose merge-splits keep
only the newer copy (Figure 3). The problem lies in the earlier
stages, where each table is routed through its own part of the
network and the two parts share each stage’s stash. The two
copies of a key follow corresponding routes in the two parts.
In the extreme case, repeated accesses leave the two tables
with exactly the same keys, and every overflow in one part
recurs in the other.
A merge’s preparation therefore routes the older table
through every stage, including the last, and the carry joins
only in the sweep (Figure 4). The two tables thus meet only
in the sweep, which removes duplicates as in Section 2.3.
Fixed capacities. How many records overflow must not show
in a rebuild’s memory trace. Every stash, range bucket, and
tier therefore has a public capacity that depends only on the
table size, and every merge-split visits its prescribed stash
storage even when no record overflows. With fixed capacities,
the scheme fails only if a record arrives at a stash, range
bucket, or tier that is already full. We implement a checker
that bounds this probability for a given configuration. For the
default configuration with 227 records, the probability that
some capacity is exceeded within 232 accesses is about 2−69.7,
below our target of 2−64. We also prove in Lean that a model
of MICRORAMA exceeds no capacity within these accesses
except with probability at most 2−69 (Appendix C). [Tianyao:

3. Deamortized Rebuilding
Without deamortization, an access of the hierarchical
ORAM of Section 2 waits for any rebuild it triggers, and
requests that arrive meanwhile queue behind it. We therefore
run rebuilds in the background while accesses continue, bal-
ancing latency, throughput, and storage overhead.
3.1. Background Rebuilding and Pre-routing
Background
rebuilding.
Following
Ostrovsky
and
Shoup [11], each level answers queries from published
tables while a background worker builds replacements.
The worker merges the partial table with each carry from
below (Section 2.3), reading published inputs and writing to
separate storage. Publishing an outgoing table passes it to
the next level as a carry.
With enough cores, each level can rebuild on its own
core. When there are fewer cores than levels, the workers
of consecutive levels share a thread. The thread runs their
merges in short pieces, in earliest-deadline-first order, so that
a long merge at a large level does not block the rebuild of
a small level. [Tianyao: Report the longest request stall with
this schedule against running each merge whole. Dev host,
32M records, one thread: 0.66 ms against 0.68–0.90 s.]
Background rebuilding adds no rebuild work, but costs
extra lookup probes and storage. Until a merge publishes its
output, every lookup must search its carry as a separate table,
and the merge’s inputs and output occupy memory at the same
time. To reduce the extra probes, we want each merge to
publish its output soon after its carry arrives.
Pre-routing. To publish sooner, a merge starts its preparation
early. Preparation does not read the carry (Section 2.4), and
the public rebuild schedule determines which hash the output
uses (Appendix B), so the merge starts preparation as soon as
the partial table is published, before its carry arrives. With this
pre-routing, only residual partitioning and the sweep remain
when the carry arrives. The top uses the same procedure.
Pre-routing also keeps the required rebuild speed close
to the average. In an idealized model where preparation
dominates the cost of a merge (Appendix B), each non-top
level at B = 8 must rebuild at 4.5× the access rate, only
about 3% faster than its average rate of 4.375×. [Tianyao:
